♻️ Green Practices

You don't need to be an expert to help the planet. Small, steady habits add up, and they start with the places you spend your day.

At school:

Print only when you need to, switch off lights and fans when a room is empty, and reuse notebooks, folders, and paper.

At home:

Buy only what you need, avoid wasting food, and use water and electricity wisely.

In daily life:

Choose reusable bottles and bags, think before you buy, and recycle where it's possible.

♻️ Sustainable Business: "Quick tips to Grow a Sustainable Business"

A sustainable business earns money while taking care of people and the planet. Here's a simple five-step roadmap to see how one grows from an idea.

Every sustainable business starts with a problem worth solving. Look at your school, your home, and your neighborhood and notice what is being wasted, polluted, or used up too quickly. Common problems include plastic waste, food waste, wasted electricity and water, and unrecyclable packaging.

Ask yourself: What bothers me about the way things are done around me, and who is affected by it?

Example: A school throws away hundreds of half-used notebooks at the end of each year.

Once you know the problem, imagine a product or service that helps solve it. A good sustainable idea does three things: it meets a real need, it reduces harm to the environment, and people are willing to pay for it or support it. Talk to the people you want to help, because their opinions can improve your idea before you spend any money.

Ask yourself: Would people actually use this, and is it better for the planet than what exists now?

Example: Collect the unused pages from old notebooks and bind them into new ones sold at the school shop.

Every business uses resources, so plan for them from the start. Think about the materials you need, the energy used to make your product, the packaging it comes in, and how it gets to customers. Look for options that are reused, recycled, local, or renewable, and think about what happens to your product when people are finished with it.

Ask yourself: Can I use less, reuse more, or choose a cleaner option at each stage?

Example: Use recycled paper, skip plastic wrapping, and deliver within the school so no transport is needed.

A great idea only lasts if it can pay for itself. A business model explains who your customers are, what they pay, what it costs you to run, and how you keep going over time. The challenge is staying profitable without giving up your environmental goals, and being honest with customers about what you do.

Ask yourself: Who will pay for this, and will the money cover my costs so the project can continue?

Example: Sell the notebooks slightly cheaper than new ones, and use part of the profit to buy more collection bins.

Good intentions are not enough, so measure what you actually achieve. Choose a simple number to track, such as kilograms of waste avoided, liters of water saved, or the number of people reached. Share the results honestly, including what didn't work, then use them to improve and grow.

Ask yourself: How will I know whether this really made a difference?

Example: Count the notebooks reused each term and report how much paper was saved.
